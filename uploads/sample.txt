The War of Troy (more commonly called the Trojan War) was a legendary ten-year siege of the city of Troy by a coalition of Greek kingdoms, sparked by the abduction (or elopement) of Helen, wife of King Menelaus of Sparta, by the Trojan prince Paris.

Core story (myth)
Cause: In the most famous version, Paris judges a beauty contest among the goddesses Hera, Athena, and Aphrodite and awards the prize to Aphrodite, who promises him the love of the most beautiful woman in the world—Helen. Helen is already married to Menelaus, so when Paris takes her to Troy, Menelaus calls on his brother Agamemnon and other Greek kings to help him get her back.

Siege: The Greeks (called Achaeans in Homer) assemble a huge fleet and besiege Troy for 10 years, with famous heroes on both sides: Achilles, Ajax, and Odysseus for the Greeks; Hector and Paris for the Trojans.

Ending – the Trojan Horse: After many battles and deaths, the Greeks pretend to sail away but leave behind a giant wooden horse as a “gift.” The Trojans bring it inside the walls; at night, Greek warriors hidden inside emerge, open the gates, and the city is sacked and destroyed.

Why it matters
It’s the central myth behind Homer’s Iliad (which focuses on Achilles’ rage near the end of the war) and influences the Odyssey and later works like Virgil’s Aeneid.

Culturally, it became the archetype of a war caused by desire, honor, and fate, and it shaped how later Greeks understood heroism, glory, and the gods’ interference in human affairs.

Historical reality (what scholars think)
Most scholars believe the story preserves a distant memory of real Late Bronze Age conflicts between Mycenaean Greeks and the city of Troy (Hisarlik, in modern Türkiye) around the 12th–13th century BCE.

Archaeology shows Troy was attacked and burned multiple times; one destruction layer (often linked to “Troy VIIa”) is dated to roughly 1150 BCE, consistent with a violent end by warfare.

The specific characters (Achilles, Helen, etc.) and many supernatural elements are mythic, but the core idea—a major Greek assault on a wealthy city controlling trade routes—likely has a historical kernel.
